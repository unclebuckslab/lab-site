## The answer

When Congress passed the Affordable Care Act in 2010, the Congressional Budget Office expected 24 million people in the new insurance marketplaces by 2019. That year, 9.8 million were enrolled and paying. For four straight years, enrollment was less than half the forecast.

Then the misses flipped. Every CBO baseline we checked from 2018 to 2023 came in below the next year's enrollment, except the 2018 one, which was close. Enrollment reached 21.0 million in 2024, the number CBO had expected for 2016, and 22.1 million in 2025.

## Why the forecasts missed

The early miss was about people. CBO's 2010 estimate expected 17 to 19 million people a year to get a subsidy from 2016 to 2019. Over those four years combined, the number who did came to 46% of that. The subsidy per person came much closer, at 90% of the forecast, so the money followed the head count: total subsidies were 42% of what CBO's numbers implied.

CBO's own review of its record says fewer people than it expected left employer coverage or the ranks of the uninsured to buy in the marketplaces. Some later changes made the early gap larger, not smaller. A 2012 Supreme Court ruling made the Medicaid expansion optional, which pushed some low-income people toward the marketplaces instead.

The later miss has a different shape. CBO's baselines are current-law projections: they assume the laws on the books stay as they are. Several of the low forecasts came before Congress enlarged the subsidies, in the American Rescue Plan (March 2021) and the Inflation Reduction Act (August 2022). Two came after those laws and still came in low. CBO says administrative actions also raised enrollment from 2021 to 2025, and that isolating their effects is difficult. How much of each miss the laws and those actions explain is not established.

The same reading applies to the high forecasts. CBO's March 2016 baseline expected 15 million people in 2017; 9.8 million enrolled. That baseline came before the 2017 tax law set the individual-mandate penalty to $0 and before federal cost-sharing payments stopped that October, and the 2017 miss came before either took effect.

## Where the money went

The bill rose in two stages. From 2016 to 2019, enrollment barely moved (10.0 million to 9.8 million), yet estimated premium tax credits rose 68%, from $39 billion to $66 billion in 2025 dollars. Premiums jumped 26% in 2018, after the cost-sharing payments stopped and insurers loaded those costs onto silver plans, and the credits absorbed them.

From 2021, the bill rose with enrollment. Credits reached $75 billion in 2021 and $134 billion in 2025, 3.4 times the 2016 figure.

## What happened when the extra help ended

The enhanced subsidies expired at the end of 2025. In February 2026, 19.2 million people were enrolled and paying. Against February 2025, that is a drop of 12% to 18%, depending on which count is used. CMS first reported February 2025 as 23.4 million and later revised it to 21.8 million; February 2026 is a first count and will likely be revised too. All of these numbers are preliminary.

Between 48 and 50 of the 50 states and DC lost enrollees, again depending on the count. HealthCare.gov states fell more than states that run their own marketplaces: 15% against 6% on the revised count, and 22.5% against 8.5% comparing first counts. CMS revised HealthCare.gov states down more, so the size of that gap depends on the count too. New Mexico rose on both counts, by 10,132 people. It is one small state.

The subsidy expiration was not the only change. New program-integrity rules took effect for 2026, and more people picked a plan but never paid for it.

CBO now projects about 17 million enrollees on average in 2026, down from 22 million in 2025, reflecting the subsidy expiration and the 2025 reconciliation act. CBO calls the near-term drop subject to "considerable uncertainty." February's count was higher than that, but a full-year average isn't out.

## Who it touches

The people in these numbers are the 22.1 million who bought insurance this way at the 2025 peak, most of them with a subsidy. On the revised count, the steepest drops this year came in Ohio and Oklahoma, at about a third, followed by Arizona and South Carolina at about 29%.

Some of the growth from 2021 to 2025 was not people gaining coverage. CMS ended credits or coverage for about 1.5 million people in its own reviews, after finding them ineligible or enrolled without their permission, and ended credits for more than 550,000 who were also on Medicaid. Outside estimates of improper enrollment run as high as 6.4 million for 2025. Those larger figures are contested, and we don't chart them.

## What we don't know

- How much of each forecast miss came from laws passed later, from administrative actions, or from CBO's assumptions about behavior.
- How the 2017, 2024 and 2025 baselines did. We don't have them yet.
- Full-year 2026 enrollment. February is one month, and it will be revised.
- Enrollment for 2014 and 2015 on the same paid-up basis. The CMS files we have start in 2016.
- Why New Mexico rose. We haven't found a primary source on its state funding yet, so we don't make the claim.

## Method and sources

- **Forecasts:** CBO cost estimate for the Affordable Care Act, March 20, 2010, Table 4. CBO baseline tables for health insurance, January 2015 to February 2026, plus March 2016. CBO, *Federal Subsidies for Health Insurance, 2026 to 2036* (July 2026). CBO, *CBO's Record of Projecting Subsidies for Health Insurance Under the ACA: 2014 to 2016*.
- **Actuals:** CMS effectuated (paid-up) enrollment, annual (as of April 21, 2026) and monthly (as of May 5, 2026), plus CMS's first-published February tables.
- **Prices:** BLS CPI-U annual averages. All totals and premiums are in 2025 dollars, except the subsidy per person, which is compared in nominal dollars because CBO forecast it that way.
- **Like with like:** actual enrollment is the average monthly number of people enrolled and paying, CBO's closest measure. CBO counts people under 65 through May 2023 and all ages from 2026; CMS counts all ages. Subsidy totals are our estimate (people with a credit × average monthly credit × 12) and exclude cost-sharing reductions. The September 2023 baseline restates May 2023 and is counted once.
- **Code and data:** every number comes from `pipeline/analysis/r001.py`, run on versioned snapshots with checksums.
- **Evidence:** Official data; chart 1 also carries the Projection tag.
